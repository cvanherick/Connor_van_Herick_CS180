Project 1

Images of the Russian Empire

Reconstructing color photographs from Prokudin-Gorskii glass plate scans by aligning the red and green exposures to the blue channel.

Approach

From Three Plates to One Color Image

Each source image stores the blue, green, and red exposures stacked vertically. I split the image into thirds, used the blue exposure as the reference channel, and searched for the displacement that maximized normalized cross-correlation. To keep borders from dominating the score, the metric is computed on the interior of each candidate alignment.

For large TIFFs, I use an image pyramid: align small versions first, double that displacement at each larger level, then refine around the estimate. This keeps the search fast while still finding large offsets. The baseline results below use raw pixel NCC; the final section shows bells and whistles that improve difficult cases.

Bells & Whistles

Edges, Cropping, and Contrast

Raw RGB intensities are not always comparable across channels. The Emir portrait is the best example: the blue exposure differs enough that plain pixel matching can choose a visibly wrong offset. As a bell and whistle, I scored alignments using simple gradient magnitude edges, then applied an interior crop and percentile contrast stretch after stacking.

Notes

Failures and Tradeoffs

The largest failure mode was relying only on raw channel brightness. Some scenes contain strong color-dependent differences, so the NCC score can prefer a displacement that matches intensity patterns but leaves colored ghosts around important edges. Edge-based scoring helps because it compares structure rather than brightness, while final cropping removes the wraparound artifacts introduced by circular shifts.
